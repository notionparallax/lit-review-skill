# Choosing methods

Two different method choices come up in a review, and people often run them together:

- **The review's own method** (Part A): what kind of review this is, which sets how sources are found, judged and combined. Choose it in stage 2.
- **The methods of the studies reviewed, and of the work that follows** (Parts B and C): which research designs the field uses, what each can and cannot show, and which design would answer the questions the review leaves open. This comes up in stages 5, 6 and 9.

Discuss both with the person, in plain words. Most practitioners have not had to name a review type before; explain the options that fit their purpose and tier, recommend one with the reason, and let them choose. Record the choice and the reason in `02_question.md`, and name it in the review's method paragraph, citing the methodological source (add it to Zotero by DOI; the DOIs below were checked against Crossref).

## Part A: what kind of review?

| Type | Answers | What it takes | Fits tier | Guidance |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Narrative review** | 'What is known about X, and how does it fit together?' Broad and interpretive | A logged search, judgement in selection, a clear argument | All | Baethge et al. (2019), SANRA: 10.1186/s41073-019-0064-8 |
| **Rapid review** | A focused practical question, answered quickly by streamlining a systematic method | A narrow question; stated shortcuts (one database, one screener with checks, date limits) | Scan, briefing | Garritty et al. (2021): 10.1016/j.jclinepi.2020.10.007 |
| **Scoping review** | 'What has been studied, how, and where are the gaps?' Maps a field; doesn't weigh findings | A broad question; systematic search; charting of study characteristics | Briefing, full | Arksey & O'Malley (2005): 10.1080/1364557032000119616; PRISMA-ScR, Tricco et al. (2018): 10.7326/M18-0850 |
| **Integrative review** | Combines empirical, theoretical and grey sources of different designs into a new understanding or framework | Explicit search; extraction and appraisal; comparison across designs | Briefing (light), full | Whittemore & Knafl (2005): 10.1111/j.1365-2648.2005.03621.x; Torraco (2005): 10.1177/1534484305278283 |
| **Systematic review** (with or without meta-analysis) | A narrow question about an effect ('Does X change Y?'), answered with every eligible study | A fixed protocol, exhaustive search, two screeners, formal appraisal; months of work | Full (publishable track) | PRISMA 2020, Page et al. (2021): 10.1136/bmj.n71 |
| **Realist review** | 'What works, for whom, in what circumstances, and why?' For complex interventions | Theory-building from mixed evidence; strong judgement | Full | Pawson et al. (2005): 10.1258/1355819054308530 |
| **Qualitative evidence synthesis** | How people experience or understand something, from qualitative studies | Thematic coding across studies | Briefing, full | Thomas & Harden (2008): 10.1186/1471-2288-8-45 |

For an overview of these and other types, Grant & Booth (2009): 10.1111/j.1471-1842.2009.00848.x. For guidance on choosing between them, Snyder (2019): 10.1016/j.jbusres.2019.07.039.

**How to recommend.** Ask what the person will do with the answer:

- They want to understand a topic, find its arguments and frame a project: **narrative**, with a logged search. This is the default for scan and briefing tiers, and is what this skill's stages produce without extra work.
- They need a defensible answer to a practical question soon: **rapid**.
- They want to know what has been studied, to plan research or find a gap: **scoping**. Stage 5 then records study characteristics more than findings.
- They want to bring different kinds of evidence (studies, theory, industry data) into one picture or framework: **integrative**.
- They want to know whether something works, and the literature has comparable studies with measured effects: **systematic**, but only on the full tier, and usually not in a practice setting.

Whatever the type, the review says which it is and does what that type promises. A review called systematic that wasn't is the commonest fault reviewers find.

## Part B: the methods of the studies reviewed

Record each study's design in `05_sources.md` ('What it is') in consistent terms, so designs can be compared. A useful set:

| Design | Can show | Can't show |
|---|---|---|
| Randomised or natural experiment | That X changes Y, in that setting | Whether it transfers; often why |
| Longitudinal / panel | Change over time; order of events | Cause, without a design that rules out alternatives |
| Cross-sectional survey | Prevalence; associations; what people say | Cause; what people actually do |
| Observed or logged data (sensors, records, transactions) | What people actually do, at scale | Why; and only what the instrument sees |
| Qualitative (interviews, ethnography, focus groups) | Meanings, reasons, mechanisms, experience | How common something is |
| Case study / post-occupancy evaluation | How something worked in one place, in depth | Generalisation |
| Simulation or modelling | Consequences of assumptions | Whether the assumptions hold |
| Review | The state of a literature | More than its sources do |
| Theory, history, law, practice guidance | Concepts, context, rules, precedent | Empirical effects |

At stage 6, summarise the **methods landscape** for each theme in a few lines: which designs dominate, what that means for how far the findings can be trusted, and where self-report and observed measures disagree. A field built mostly on cross-sectional surveys can describe associations but not causes, and the review should say so. This summary often becomes part of the 'what is not known' paragraph of the spine.

## Part C: methods for the work that follows

Most reviews in practice exist to inform something: a design, a study, a pilot, a brief. At stage 6 (in the gap list) and again at stage 9, discuss with the person what kind of study would close each gap that matters to them. For each gap, set out:

- the question in a form a study could answer;
- the design that would answer it (from the table in Part B), and why that design and not a cheaper one;
- what is feasible in their setting: access to people, sites, data; time; budget; skills;
- ethics and privacy constraints (consent, monitoring of people, data about staff or clients);
- what a cheaper, weaker version would still show, and what it wouldn't.

Offer this as a discussion, not a verdict; the person knows their constraints. Record the outcome in `06_outline.md` (gap list) and, if they want it in the review, a short 'implications for research and practice' section.
