# Stage 2: The question

**Purpose.** Turn the germ of the idea into a question a search can answer, with clear edges. Most of the trouble later (searches that miss, sections that sprawl, an argument that drifts) comes from a question that was never pinned down.

**Output.** `02_question.md`.

## What to work through with the person

Do this as a conversation, a few questions at a time. Offer drafts for them to react to; most people find it easier to correct a draft question than to write one.

1. **The question in one sentence.** Draft two or three versions of different breadth and let them pick or blend. A good review question names a population, a phenomenon and an outcome or angle: "What is known about how office workers choose which days to come in, and why?" rather than "Hybrid work".
2. **Sub-questions.** Two to five, which will usually become the sections or themes. At scan tier, two or three.
3. **Population and setting.** Who and where. Geography: which countries' evidence transfers to their context? Treat this as a rough guide to applicability, not a hard exclusion; useful studies from elsewhere can be included and marked as outside scope.
4. **Time span.** From when? Is there a break point (for example, before and after 2020)?
5. **Kinds of evidence.** Empirical studies only, or also theory, history, law, practice guidance, vendor and industry reports? Industry reports are often the only data in practice-facing fields; they can be used as data, with the publisher's interest stated (see [verification.md](verification.md)).
6. **What is deliberately out.** Name neighbouring topics that will be left out, and why. This is what keeps the review from sprawling. Write it as a scope note that can go into the review's introduction.
7. **What they already believe.** Ask directly: "What do you expect the literature to say?" Record their hunches as hypotheses. This is not to confirm them; it tells you which contrary evidence to search for hardest.
8. **Seed papers.** Papers, reports, authors or books they already know and trust. Even three or four help a lot: they test whether the search strings work (stage 3) and are the starting points for following references.
9. **Their own stake.** Does the person or their organisation have work, a product, a project or data that the review bears on? If so, record it, agree whether the review mentions it, and keep the review's conclusions independent of it. Do not cite or draw on unpublished internal work unless they say to.
10. **'As at' date.** For anything time-sensitive (law, pending policy, market figures), agree the date the review speaks as at.
11. **What kind of review.** Once the question is settled, read Part A of [methods.md](methods.md) and talk through the review types that fit their purpose and tier. Recommend one, with the reason, and record the choice in `02_question.md`. The type decides what later stages emphasise: a scoping review records study characteristics more than findings; an integrative review needs an extraction table; a rapid review states its shortcuts.

## Check before closing

- Is the question answerable by literature, or is it really a question for their own data or a design decision? Say so if it is the latter; a review can still frame it.
- Does the scope fit the tier from stage 1? A briefing cannot cover five literatures properly. If it doesn't fit, propose cutting sub-questions rather than thinning everything.
- Read the question back in plain words and ask whether it is what they want to know.

## Gate

They agree the question, sub-questions, scope note and hypotheses. Update `STATUS.md` and commit.
