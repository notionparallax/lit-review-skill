# Writing the review

If a `house-style` skill is available in this session, load it before drafting: it governs tone. These rules cover what is particular to a literature review, and stand in for a house style if there isn't one.

## Register

Measured, specific and honest about how much is known. State the finding, give the number, draw the implication that can be defended, and stop. The register is a good briefing paper, not a sales document or an essay competition: no hooks, no recaps, no closing flourishes, no forward summaries ("The next section shows..."), no punchy short sentences opening paragraphs. Fold the point into a sentence that carries content.

## Rules particular to reviews

- **Every named study states what it found.** A bare citation after a name ('Smith et al. (2022) also studied this') is a fault. Give the design or sample where it bears on the weight, then the finding, then (if it isn't obvious) why it bears on the argument, in the same sentence or the next.
- **Match the breadth of a claim to the breadth of the evidence.** One study of one firm supports 'in one firm', not 'workers'. Say how many studies, firms or countries, not 'some' or 'several'.
- **Numbers say what they are a share of.** 'Forty per cent of those already online at 6 am', not 'forty per cent of users'. If two sources use different denominators or definitions, say so before comparing them.
- **Observed and reported are different evidence.** Say which a finding rests on when it matters (survey answers against sensor or log data).
- **Interested sources are named as such** at first citation (see [verification.md](verification.md)). Where interested and independent sources address the same question, set them side by side.
- **Gloss specialist terms once, at first use**, in plain words, using the term the reader's field uses.
- **Refer back to studies by citation** (`@key`), not by description ('the Dutch experiment'), which makes the reader hunt.
- **Give agency to whoever acts.** 'Workers who choose their days...', not 'the choice has consequences'. Abstract nouns acting as subjects ('the evidence wants', 'the data tell') read as vague.
- **Prefer neutral wording on contested questions** ('most contested', not 'weakest').
- **Mark the drafter's inferences** as inferences.
- **Repeat the noun** rather than a pronoun whose antecedent is far back. Avoid gendered pronouns for authors where it's easy: use the name.

## Conventions (defaults; `01_scope.md` may change them)

- Australian English: -ise, -our, -re; single quotation marks with punctuation outside unless it belongs to the quote; no full stops in abbreviations (US, Dr); dates as 15 July 2026; Oxford comma; 'per cent' in prose, '%' in tables and with figures in parentheses.
- '≈' for 'approximately' with numbers, never '~'.
- Few em dashes. Use commas, colons, semicolons or parentheses.
- Reference list in APA 7, built from Zotero; don't edit or 'Australianise' titles in references.

## Words to avoid

'notably', 'importantly', 'crucially', 'interestingly', 'it is worth noting', 'clearly', 'in order to', 'leverage', 'utilise', 'holistic', 'going forward', 'stakeholders', 'best practice', 'underscores', 'landscape', 'delve', 'navigate', 'moreover', 'furthermore', 'matters' used as a hand-wave ('this matters'; say what follows from it instead). Avoid the 'not X, but Y' construction and pseudo-clefts ('What the evidence shows is...'). `style_check.py` flags most of these.
